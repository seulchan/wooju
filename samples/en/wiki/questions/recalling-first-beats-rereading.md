# Recalling a note first beats rereading it

## Insight

A note that I try to recall before reopening it stays in memory far longer than a note I simply reread. The attempt to recall, not the time spent reading, is what keeps it.

## Why it may matter

If this holds, the way to keep what I read is not to read more carefully but to close the note and try to say it back. That is cheap, and it tells me which notes I have actually kept.

## Evidence

- In [a week of recalling notes](../../sources/a-week-of-recalling-notes.md), recalled notes came back nearly complete by the third attempt; reread-only notes felt unread.
- The [forgetting curve](../../sources/forgetting-curve.md): each revisit slows the next decline, which fits the week's pattern of easier recall each day.
- [Spaced repetition](../../sources/spaced-repetition.md) systems are built around recall attempts with growing gaps, not around rereading.

## Challenge

- The week's notes are a diary of one person who chose which notes to recall; the recalled notes may have been the easier ones.
- The forgetting curve was measured with nonsense syllables and says nothing about recall versus rereading.
- The effect might fade for long or complex notes, where a recall attempt covers only a fragment.

## Boundaries

Holds so far for short notes (a summary and a few points). Untested for long notes and for material I did not write myself.
